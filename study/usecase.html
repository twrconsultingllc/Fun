Concept overview · not a live product

A licensing team that tracks the rules — and every client's paperwork — so nothing falls through the cracks

An assistant system built for a licensing consulting firm: it does the first pass of state-by-state money transmitter licensing research automatically, and it keeps a running case file for every client so your team always knows exactly where each one stands.

Please read before anything else This is a decision-support tool, not a substitute for licensed compliance or legal review. It does not make licensing determinations and it is not a source of current regulatory figures or a system of record for real client data. Every recommendation it produces should be verified by your compliance team against NMLS and each state's own regulator before you act on it.
The problem

Two things get lost once a firm has more than a handful of clients

Every state has its own money transmitter licensing rules — bonding requirements, minimum net worth, review timelines, exemptions. Researching each one, keeping the findings organized, and turning that into something a client or a regulator can act on is repetitive and easy to get inconsistent between states. That's only half the job, though. The other half is tracking where each client actually stands — which states they're already licensed in, what's still open on an in-progress application, whose ownership just changed and needs a fresh filing. Past a handful of clients, that tracking lives in scattered spreadsheets and email threads, and losing track of a client's status is just as costly as misremembering a state's bonding requirement.

The solution

A small team of assistants, each with one job

Instead of one person doing everything by hand, five narrowly-scoped assistants each handle one part of the process — the way you'd split the work across a research analyst, a case manager, a checklist owner, a writer, and a second reviewer.

🔎

Research Assistant

Looks up what a given state requires — bonding, net worth, typical review time — instantly, instead of someone searching state by state.

🗂️

Client Tracker

Keeps one running case file per client — who they are, what they do, and exactly where each state application stands — so status lives in one place instead of a dozen inboxes.

📋

Checklist Builder

Turns a target state and a client's actual profile into one clear go/no-go packet: what's covered, what's missing, and what still needs real research.

✍️

Memo Writer

Drafts a short, readable summary memo from the checklist — the kind of write-up you'd hand to a client or put in front of legal.

🛡️

Second-Look Reviewer

Checks the checklist and memo for completeness before a human ever sees it — every target state accounted for, nothing silently skipped.

What it's actually for

Three kinds of questions it answers

A consulting firm doesn't ask one kind of question — it asks these three, over and over, about different clients at different stages:

  • A brand-new client: "Would this prospect qualify for a license in Washington today, and what's missing?"
  • An existing client with a change: "This client is bringing on a new part-owner — which of their in-progress applications need updating?"
  • A client mid-application: "What's still open on this client's Florida application, and is anything overdue?"
Why it's worth it

What you get

  • A consistent first draft for every state, instead of whatever one researcher happened to find that week
  • One case file per client instead of a spreadsheet per analyst, so everyone sees the same status
  • Far less manual lookup time before your compliance team's real review even starts
  • A packet that's already been checked once before it reaches a person — nothing obviously missing
  • A clear, honest "we don't know yet" whenever a state or a client detail falls outside what the assistants have on file, instead of a guess dressed up as an answer
The real-world question

What this would actually cost to run

The honest answer, worked out from the technical version of this project: not much, at least not in day-to-day usage costs. A single question — checking a client's status, running a gap analysis — costs a small fraction of a cent at today's published AI-model pricing. Running it for a firm handling a few dozen clients, with analysts asking it a few thousand questions a month, comes out to roughly the cost of one modest team lunch per month, not a line item that needs its own budget conversation.

Hosting the assistant itself is similarly small — it doesn't need specialized infrastructure, just a small always-on service, or one that only runs when someone actually asks it something.

These are estimates from a technical study project, using published list prices at the time they were written — not a quote. Real usage patterns and provider choice would need to be checked before anyone builds a budget around them. But at this order of magnitude, cost was never going to be the reason a project like this doesn't happen.
What this is not

Judgment still belongs to your team

These assistants produce a first draft, not a final answer. They don't replace your compliance officers, your outside counsel, or your regulators — they take the repetitive research and status tracking off your team's plate so the people with real judgment can spend their time on the calls that actually need it. Every packet they produce should be reviewed by a qualified person and verified against current NMLS and state-regulator sources before anyone acts on it, and every client record it holds should carry only what the licensing work actually needs, with real access controls around anything closer to genuine client data than this concept's fictitious samples.
